65 · RLHF & RL Deep Dive

Explain it to a 12-year-old Imagine a dog that learned to say words, but only picks whatever word sounds most likely next — it doesn't care if the sentence is useful or nice. RLHF is the training where a human says "that was a good sentence, here's a treat" or "that was bad, no treat". The dog slowly learns which sentences get treats and starts saying those instead. The deep dive is about exactly how the treats are counted and how the dog is pushed toward them.

Intuition

A pretrained model is trained to predict the most likely next token. That makes it fluent, but "most likely" is not the same as "helpful". A base model will happily continue a harmful prompt, refuse nothing, and answer with whatever is statistically probable rather than what a person actually wants. RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) is the layer that turns "fluent text guesser" into "helpful assistant".

The core idea is simple: collect human judgments about which answers are good, turn those judgments into a numeric reward, and then train the model to maximize that reward. The hard part is doing all of that without breaking the model's language ability — which is where the technical machinery comes in.

What happens technically

The RLHF pipeline has three stages:

  1. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) — train the base model on hand-written, high-quality instruction–response pairs so it learns the format of being helpful.
  2. Reward model — collect pairs of answers to the same prompt, have humans rank them (A better than B), and train a small model to predict that preference as a scalar score.
  3. Policy optimization (PPO) — use the reward model as the signal to push the SFT model's weights so it produces answers that score higher.

Why PPO, and what does it actually do? If we simply updated the weights to maximize reward, the model would quickly collapse into repeating the few highest-scoring answers and lose its diversity and fluency. PPO is an on-policy reinforcement-learning algorithm that makes small, stable updates. Two details matter for a high-level understanding:

  • The reward signal — the reward model's score tells the policy how good an answer was, but the score is only as good as the human preferences behind it.
  • The KL penalty — PPO adds a penalty proportional to how far the new policy drifts from the reference (SFT) model. This keeps the model from over-optimizing the reward and destroying its language ability. The reward and the KL penalty are traded off against each other.

DPO — the simpler alternative

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) removes the separate reward model and the PPO loop entirely. Instead, it rewrites the RL objective into a simple classification loss: for each pair (preferred answer, rejected answer), the model is trained to assign higher probability to the preferred one. This is much simpler and more stable to train, and for many tasks it matches or beats RLHF.

Why this helps me (as a code user)

You never run RLHF yourself, but it explains the behavior you see in every frontier model. Why does a model refuse certain requests, apologize, hedge, or seem eager to please? Because its weights were shaped by human preferences, not just by next-token statistics. Knowing this changes how you use models: you understand that "alignment" is a trained property with trade-offs, that a model's style is a learned artifact, and that prompting is partly steering a behavior that RLHF baked in. It also tells you which knob you can actually turn — fine-tuning for SFT-style control, and prompt design for nudging the aligned behavior — versus the alignment itself, which is fixed at training time.
