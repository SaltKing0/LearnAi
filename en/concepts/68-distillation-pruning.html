68 · Distillation & Pruning

Explain it to a 12-year-old A huge encyclopedia is great, but too heavy to carry around. Distillation is like a clever author reading that encyclopedia and writing a small pocket book that captures the most important ideas in their own words. Pruning is like trimming a big tree: you cut off the branches that do almost nothing, and the tree still stands — just lighter and easier to move.

Intuition

Frontier models are enormous — hundreds of billions of parameters — and that makes them slow and expensive. But you rarely need the whole brain. Knowledge distillation trains a small "student" model to copy the behavior of a large "teacher" model, so the student ends up with much of the teacher's ability in a fraction of the size. Pruning takes an existing model and removes weights or neurons that contribute little, leaving a leaner network behind.

What happens technically

Knowledge distillation: instead of only learning from labeled data, the student also trains to match the teacher's outputs — often the raw probability distributions (logits) before the final softmax. Those soft, informative targets ("this word is probably the answer, but that other one is close too") carry far more signal than a single hard label, so a small model can learn surprisingly well. The student is typically a smaller or shallower architecture trained on the teacher's predictions.

Pruning: many weights in a trained network are near zero or barely influence the output. Pruning identifies these (by magnitude, by gradient, or by how much removing them changes the loss) and removes them — either setting them to zero (sparse) or removing entire neurons/channels (structured). After pruning, the model is usually fine-tuned a little to recover lost accuracy.

Both differ from quantization: quantization changes the precision of numbers (fewer bits), while distillation builds a smaller architecture and pruning removes parts of the architecture. The trade-off is the same: smaller and faster, but some quality loss.

Why this helps me (as a code user)

Distilled and pruned models are how frontier-quality behavior gets onto edge devices, laptops, and budget deployments. As a code user you rarely write the distillation yourself — instead you pick a distilled or pruned variant from a model hub (for example a "distill" or "small" checkpoint) and serve it. It gives you lower latency, lower cost, and a smaller memory footprint for a small quality hit, which is often the right engineering trade-off.
